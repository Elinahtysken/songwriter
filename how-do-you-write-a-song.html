How to write a song

By Elina Tyskling

One day a phrase might get attached to the window of your perception. Or the word or phrase might cut a little wound open in the day-to-day matrix we cling to.

A phrase that, as you say it a few times, makes you want to hear it in a song, makes you want to mantrafy it. Elevate its power. It might fill you with some alacrity to put it to paper. That’s the beginning of a song.

That’s the start of a treasure hunt. The treasure hunt is not about writing the best you can; it's about finding out what words lie under the mud of the clichés, which is muddied, if you will, by these things that we say.

Underneath lie pictures, emotions, things that need to be said.

It's not a process in the sense that if you just follow a few steps, you end up at the result. It’s a world-bending activity.

I usually start with the verse, but sometimes I start with the chorus.

Start where the song begins for you. That's your keyhole, that's the crack in reality. That’s the gift from above.

I have a fondness for rhymes, rhythms, and consonance. But it does not have to rhyme. If it doesn't, be prepared to face resistance from people's ears.

I usually play around with rhymes and what I really wanna say before I think about sentence lengths.

The important thing is to say exactly what you want to say. And don't be shy; the dirtiest images of your mind or your life are what should be in the song.

Sometimes you might want the song to have a logic to the length of sentences.

Like:

I choose words you shouldn't say
I phrase my love in an intimidating way

But I lie on you like morning dew
My eyes hold fire but they're soft and blue

But depending on the melody, you can play around with the lengths. I have found this magical property of sentences: they seem to limit the melodic possibilities by themselves. Someone much more educated in writing might be able to tell us why that is.

That being said, when you have your song written down, you find that it’s easier to find the melody.

Then we have the chorus. A paroxysm. A prayer, a call, a cry. The chorus needs to be good; otherwise, start over.

I usually prefer some type of drastic change between verse and melody. The melody needs to be anticipated; otherwise, it doesn't hit you. It needs to create a craving for it. A lust.

We don’t shy away in the chorus, we expose the bare truth.

So we have come to the melody. This is sometimes, and for some people, like myself, the scary part. It's the part where you end up in aporia, in a state of feeling gutted by your utter uselessness.

I suggest not going for the first melody that pops into your head. I suggest sleeping on it. That’s at least what I do. I sing and play the piano, and try to find something that works. And then I sleep on it.

On rare occasions, I go back to the first ideas, but usually the best one comes a little later.

I can’t tell you how to do it other than just try different ones. Think about what song you are writing. And make that song. It's easy to lose yourself. Don't sweat similarities to other songs; that's just the reality of songmaking.

I happen to have vocal cords that don't easily bend to my will, so I record myself singing all the time, to make sure to catch the time when I get it just right. Listen to yourself singing it. Make sure that the song is somewhat coherent and tells the same story.

So have we finally created a song? If you are anything like me, you will find little parts of the song that itch in a bad way; my soul is telling me they are wrong. Most likely others won't exactly feel that, but they will feel it when you get it right.

Trust those itches and correct what needs correcting. And one other thing: not all songs will be your best, but they can still be important.

When you're in a state of dejection and sit with the reality that maybe your songs, even the best ones, might never be heard or produced, you can at least take comfort in knowing that you're in good company.

Go on, embark on your treasure hunt.

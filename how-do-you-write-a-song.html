How to write a song

By Elina Tyskling

One day a phrase might get attached to the window of your perception. It might cut a little wound open in the day-to-day matrix we cling to. A phrase whose power you want to elevate. You might feel excited to put it to paper.

That's the beginning of a song.

That's the start of a treasure hunt. It's not about writing the best song you can. It's about finding out what words or images lie under the mud of the clichés.

It's not a process that, in a few steps, takes you to the finish line. It is a world-bending activity, if you believe in the power of words and music, that is.

I usually start with the verse, but sometimes I start with the chorus. Start where the song begins for you. That's your keyhole. That's the crack in reality.

I have a fondness for rhymes, rhythms, and consonance. But it does not have to rhyme. If it doesn't, be prepared for some resistance from people's ears. I usually play around with rhymes and what I really wanna say before I think about sentence lengths. The important thing is to say exactly what you want to say. And don't be shy. The most uniquely personal images you hold in your mind are exactly what you should put in the text.

Sometimes you might want the song to have a logic to the length of sentences.

Like:

I choose words you shouldn't say
I phrase my love in an intimidating way
But I lie on you like morning dew
My eyes hold fire but they're soft and blue

But depending on the melody, you can play around with the lengths. I have found this magical property of sentences: they seem to limit the melodic possibilities by themselves. Someone much more educated in writing might be able to tell us why that is. Still, when you have your song written down, you find that it's easier to come up with the melody.

Then we have the chorus. A paroxysm. A prayer, a call, a cry. The chorus needs to be good. Otherwise, start over. I usually prefer a drastic shift between verse and chorus. The chorus needs to be anticipated. Or it loses its impact. Nothing in the chorus shall ring falsely, there should be no daylight between the truth and the words. Don't dress it up nicely.

So we have come to the melody. The scary part. It's the part where you end up in aporia, just fumbling around. Maybe you even feel useless. Taunted by the black-and-white grin in front of you.

I suggest you don't go for the first melody that pops into your head. I suggest sleeping on it. That's at least what I do. I sing and play the piano and try to find something that works. And then I sleep on it. On rare occasions, I go back to the first ideas, but usually the best one comes a little later.

I can't tell you how to do it other than to try different melodies. Think about what song you are writing. What sounds should your story make? And make that song. It's easy to lose yourself. Don't sweat if you notice some similarities between your song and others. That's just the reality of songmaking.

I happen to have vocal cords that don't easily bend to my will, so I record myself singing all the time to make sure to catch the time when I get it just right. Listen to yourself singing it. Make sure that the song is somewhat coherent and tells the same story.

So have we finally created a song? If you are anything like me, you will find little parts of the song that itch in a bad way. Most likely, others won't exactly feel that, but they will feel it when you get it right. Trust those itches and correct what needs correcting. And one other thing: not all songs will be your best, but they can still be important.

And don’t let AI ruin the you-ness of the things you create. It's meant for that. Flatten humanity into a grainy sweet nothingness.

When you're in a state of dejection and sit with the reality that maybe your songs, even the best ones, might never be heard or produced, you can at least take comfort in knowing that you're in good company.

Go on, embark on your treasure hunt.
